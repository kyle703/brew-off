**Handoff document for blog post development.**

Brewed late September 2026, Richmond VA. Kegged Sunday October 4. Fully carbonated by October 11.

## The beer

Triple Crossing Brewing in Richmond makes an IPA called Falcon Smash. This is a clone of it, rethemed for Virginia Tech.

**Hazy-leaning West Coast IPA.** Heavy on Falconer's Flight hops, which taste like orange marmalade and stone fruit. Real bitterness, not a milkshake. Oats and wheat in the grain bill for body and haze.

### The name

Three jokes stacked on each other.

1. **Falconer's Flight → Falcon → Captain Falcon.** The Super Smash Bros character whose signature move is a flaming fist punch. "Hokie Smash" is a Smash Bros pun.
2. **SMaSH is a brewing term** meaning Single Malt and Single Hop. Falcon Smash is not a SMaSH. It has multiple malts and multiple hops. Triple Crossing named it that anyway. This clone keeps the joke.
3. **Hokie** is the Virginia Tech mascot. Blacksburg relocation of a Richmond beer.

**Maroon stays in the branding and out of the beer.** Every ingredient that turns beer maroon (hibiscus, elderberry) makes an IPA taste worse. This was considered and rejected.

## Recipe as brewed

### Grain, 15 lb total

| Malt | Amount | Price | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Briess 2-Row | 11 lb | $1.29/lb | $14.19 |
| Briess White Wheat | 2 lb | $1.49/lb | $2.98 |
| Flaked Oats | 1.5 lb | $1.99/lb | $2.99 |
| Rice Hulls | 0.5 lb | $2.39/lb | $1.20 |

About $21 total from Original Gravity. Bought uncrushed and milled at home at a 0.035 inch gap.

Flaked oats and rice hulls do not get milled. Rice hulls are inert, add no flavor, and exist only to keep the bed draining. With 2 lb of wheat and 1.5 lb of oats the grain bed turns gummy, and the hulls are cheap insurance at $1.20.

The 11 lb of 2-row was already half a pound above a standard recipe to compensate for stirring the mash instead of recirculating it. In hindsight that was not nearly enough. See Learnings.

### Hops, 12 oz total

| When | Hops |
|---|---|
| Start of 60 min boil | 0.75 oz Columbus |
| Flameout at 170F, 20 min steep | 3 oz Falconer's Flight, 1 oz Centennial |
| Day 3 dry hop | 5 oz Falconer's Flight, 1 oz Centennial, 1.25 oz Columbus |

Totals by variety: Falconer's Flight 8 oz, Centennial 2 oz, Columbus 2 oz.

**All bitterness comes from the boil addition.** Everything after flameout is aroma and flavor only. Below 170F hop acids stop converting to bitterness.

Flameout hops went in **loose, inside the empty grain basket**, which acts as a giant strainer. The basket comes out after the spent grain is dumped, gets rinsed, and goes back in.

Dry hops went in **completely loose, no bags**, in two or three pours to avoid a foam surge up the blow-off tube. 7.25 oz of pellets hitting an active fermentation at once will climb.

### Yeast

**One packet (11 g) Lallemand Verdant IPA**, sprinkled dry across the surface. No rehydration, no starter.

The original plan called for two packets. The homebrew shop said one was enough for this gravity, and they were right. 87% attenuation is proof it was never underpitched.

### Water

Built from distilled water, so there is no alkalinity to fight and no guesswork about the municipal supply.

**Mash: 4 gallon jugs plus 2 cups**

| Addition | Amount |
|---|---|
| Calcium chloride | 5 g |
| Gypsum | 2.5 g |
| Epsom salt | 1 g |
| Lactic acid 88% | 2 mL |

**Rinse: 3 gallon jugs**

| Addition | Amount |
|---|---|
| Calcium chloride | 3 g |
| Gypsum | 1.5 g |
| Epsom salt | 1 g |
| Lactic acid 88% | 1 mL |

Distilled water on its own makes beer taste thin and flat. These minerals are what make hops taste like hops. Calcium chloride pushes toward a rounder, fuller mouthfeel, gypsum pushes toward a crisper, more bitter edge. The ratio here leans toward the round side, which suits a hazy-leaning IPA.

The lactic acid is optional. With distilled water the mash lands near pH 5.6 on its own and the acid only pulls it to 5.4.

### Cut from the recipe

Worth mentioning in the post, since the cuts have reasons.

- **El Dorado hops.** Planned, never bought. Falconer's Flight is itself a blend and already covers that tropical note. Dropping it simplified the schedule and changed nothing.
- **Murphy & Rude Virginia malt.** A nice local-sourcing angle for a Virginia-themed beer, but too expensive to justify on a first all-grain batch where the odds of wasting it were real.
- **Orange zest at flameout.** Suggested, then rejected. Falconer's Flight already tastes like orange marmalade. Adding zest would have been redundant at best and soapy at worst.
- **Maroon-colored ingredients.** Hibiscus and elderberry both turn beer maroon and both taste wrong in an IPA. The theme lives on the label instead.

## What went wrong

This is the most useful section for a blog post. Each of these has a clean cause and a clean fix.

### 1. Mash efficiency came in at 51% against a 72% target

The single biggest miss of the batch. Preboil gravity read 1.050 instead of 1.061.

Possible causes, in order of likelihood: the crush was too coarse for a basket system, the rinse water was poured too fast and channeled through the bed instead of washing it, or stirring did not distribute heat as evenly as assumed and part of the bed never fully converted.

**Decision made mid-brew:** boil the full 60 minutes, skip the reserved half gallon of top-up water, and concentrate rather than dilute. An optional half pound of table sugar was offered to make up the alcohol and was declined, which was the right call. Sugar would have thinned the body on a beer already short of it.

Result: OG 1.060 instead of 1.071.

### 2. The Foundry's gallon markings stop at 5.5

Preboil volume was supposed to be 6.1 gallons. There is no way to measure that in the kettle, because the etched marks run out. Volume ended up below plan and essentially unmeasured.

**Fix for next time:** make a dipstick. A sanitized paddle or a length of dowel marked at known volumes, calibrated by pouring in measured gallons of water before brew day.

### 3. The mash was physically unmanageable

Not a flavor problem, a labor problem, and the worst part of the day.

15 lb of grain in the basket came close to overflowing it. With the bed that full there was no headroom to move grain around, and stirring a thick wheat-and-oat mash in a packed basket is genuinely hard work. **The brew spoon visibly bent under the load.**

This connects directly to the efficiency miss. A bed you cannot stir properly is a bed that does not convert evenly, and the no-pump decision assumed stirring would be easy enough to do well every 15 minutes. At this grain weight it was not.

**Fixes for next time, in order of preference:**

- A proper mash paddle, stainless or heavy wood. The bending spoon is undersized for this volume.
- Drop the grain bill or split the batch. 15 lb is at or past the practical ceiling for this basket.
- Revisit the recirculation pump. It was skipped on the theory that a spoon gets most of the way there. At this grain weight the spoon could not.

### 4. The grain bed drained slowly

Predictable with 2 lb of wheat and 1.5 lb of oats, even with rice hulls.

**Do not squeeze the bag or press the grain.** Squeezing extracts tannins, which taste like over-steeped tea and dry the back of your throat. Instead: stir the top of the bed, lift and reseat the basket, and stop at the volume mark regardless of how much is left in the grain.

### 5. The fermenter would not hold pressure and it emptied the CO2 tank

Tank read full, connected at 1 psi for the crash, found empty roughly two hours later at 11:30 PM.

**Cause: the fermenter was leaking.** The regulator kept feeding gas into a vessel that would not hold it, and ran the tank dry. An audible buzz during the crash was initially written off as regulator chatter, which is a real phenomenon at 1 psi, but the tank emptying settles the argument. It was a leak.

Worth writing honestly, because the initial reasoning was wrong in an instructive way. The assumption was that a CO2 gauge reading full means nothing, since CO2 sits in the tank as a liquid and the high pressure gauge reads around 800 psi until the last of the liquid boils off. That is true and still worth knowing. It was not what happened here.

**Diagnostic that should have been run immediately:** brush Star San or soapy water around the lid gasket, the elbow fitting, the manifold joint, and the racking arm stem, and watch for growing bubbles. Under positive pressure every imperfect seal is an exit.

**Two lessons:**

- **Leak test the fermenter before it matters.** Pressurize to 1 psi with no beer in it and find the leak on a day when nothing is at stake.
- **Buy a second tank.** Running dry mid-transfer with a full fermenter is the actual disaster. Two small tanks beat one large one.

### Gas resupply cost

Replacement gas ran about **$400 at Bob's Oxygen**. Most of that was the nitrogen regulator and tank, which are a separate purchase for a different purpose and not strictly part of this batch. **CO2 exchanges are about $30 each**, which is the number that matters for ongoing cost.

For the post, this is the real punchline on the dead tank: a two-hour leak at 1 psi turned into a $400 Saturday.

### 6. Air got into the beer during the 48 hour cold crash

Direct consequence of the dead tank. With no CO2 feeding the fermenter, cooling beer contracts and pulls air in through whatever seal leaks first.

**The scale of it:** cooling 5 gallons from 70F to 38F pulls in roughly 8 to 10 ounces of volume. Air is 21% oxygen, so that is about 2 ounces of oxygen gas. A real hit for a hazy IPA, but far less than a sloppy open transfer, and it came in cold, which slows the reaction considerably.

**Expected consequence:** the hop character will fade sooner than it should. Expect the citrus to drop off around week three or four instead of week five or six. The color may drift slightly darker. It will not taste like wet cardboard in week one.

The honest framing for the post: this is a recoverable mistake that costs shelf life, not a ruined batch.

### 7. A buzzing noise at 1 psi, misdiagnosed

A buzz during the crash was attributed to regulator chatter. Regulators genuinely do chatter at 1 psi, because the diaphragm hunts when the spring has almost no tension on it, and it is normally harmless. That is what made it an easy thing to dismiss.

It was the leak. The tank emptying two hours later proved it.

**The diagnostic that separates them:** put a hand on the regulator body. Buzz felt at the regulator is chatter. Buzz at the fermenter is a seal. Then brush soapy water or Star San around the gasket, elbow, manifold joint, and racking arm stem and look for bubbles. Do this rather than reasoning about which it probably is.

## What worked

Short section, but worth having, because the post should not read as pure disaster.

**Loose dry hops were a non-issue.** 7.25 oz of pellets went in with no bags at all, which is the thing most guides warn against. Clarity came out mostly fine and the transfer was not fouled. The rotating racking arm is what makes this safe: you draw from above the debris layer rather than from the floor of the vessel. On a bucket-and-siphon setup this would have been a bad idea.

**The grain basket as a flameout hop strainer worked.** No clogging at the Foundry outlet valve.

**Two-stage chilling hit pitching temperature without drama.**

**One packet of yeast was correct**, and 87% attenuation says it was never close to underpitched.

## Wanted for next time

Equipment gaps identified during this batch.

**In-line mesh filter.** Would sit in the transfer line between fermenter and keg and catch hop debris on the way through. Not bought yet because the tubing on hand is the wrong diameter to fit it. Buy the filter and the matching tubing together rather than separately.

**Wider transfer tubing.** The line used was too narrow and the transfer was slower than it needed to be. Flow through a tube scales steeply with diameter, so going up one size makes a much larger difference than shortening the run. This also solves the filter fitting problem if the sizes are chosen to match.

**A heavier mash paddle.** The current brew spoon bent.

**A volume dipstick** for the Foundry, calibrated with measured water, since the etched markings stop at 5.5 gallons.

**A second CO2 tank.**

## Process corrections, and the reasoning behind them

These are places where the initial plan was wrong and got rewritten mid-project. They are the most quotable material in the whole project, because each one is a case of confident advice meeting a physical object.

### Cold crash in the fermenter, not the keg

**Original plan:** transfer warm, then chill in the keg. The reasoning was that cooling beer in a plastic bucket pulls room air backward through the airlock.

**What changed:** the switch from a plastic bucket to the SS Brewtech fermenter, which has a sealed lid and a gas port. Once you can feed CO2 into the fermenter, crashing in the fermenter is strictly better.

**Why it matters:** yeast and hop debris drop out of suspension while the beer is cold. Crash first and all of that stays behind in the fermenter when you rack. Transfer first and you carry it into the keg, where it sits in your serving vessel for the life of the beer.

### Do not drill a spigot into a fermenter bucket

An early suggestion was to drill a bottling-bucket spigot into the fermenter for a closed transfer. The homebrew shop staff called this nonsense and they were correct.

A spigot sits at the very bottom of the bucket, which is exactly where the trub settles. It would clog immediately and take forever to pass 5 gallons. The rotating racking arm on the SS Brewtech exists precisely to solve this: you position the intake above the sediment layer, draw clear beer, and only rotate down to the floor at the very end.

### Do not trap the hop bag string under the lid gasket

Another early suggestion: tie dry hop bags to a string and trap the end under the lid gasket so they hang mid-beer.

**This breaks the CO2 seal.** Anything running through the gasket is a leak path. The corrected approach is loose hops, or weighted bags resting on the bottom.

### Loose hops do not clog an immersion chiller

An early warning about loose flameout hops clogging a plate chiller did not apply, because the setup uses an immersion chiller, which sits in the kettle and has nothing to clog. The real reason to contain the flameout hops is the Foundry's outlet valve, which pellet sludge will plug on the way out.

### The Foundry drains from the bottom

An early instruction said to drain from the top. It drains from the bottom through an outlet valve. This made the grain basket more useful rather than less, because the basket keeps hop debris away from that valve.

### Hops never need sanitizing

Pellets are kilned, compressed, and naturally antimicrobial. They go in straight from the bag. The containers and tools touching them need sanitizing, the hops do not.

## Principles worth writing up

Reusable material. These held up throughout.

### Clean versus sanitize

Two different products doing two different jobs. Cleaning removes residue. Sanitizing kills what is left on an already clean surface. **Star San on a dirty surface does nothing. You cannot sanitize soil.**

**The rule that decides every item:** the boil kills whatever is in the kettle. Anything that touches wort before or during the boil needs cleaning only. Anything that touches wort after the boil needs cleaning and then sanitizing.

**Clean only:** kettle, grain basket, support rods, lid, mash paddle, mill and grain bucket, rinse pot, measuring cups and scale, immersion chiller (it goes into the boil for the last 15 minutes, which sanitizes it).

**Clean then sanitize:** fermenter interior, lid, gasket and clamps, racking arm interior, blow-off elbow and tubing, the jar the tubing sits in, Foundry outlet valve and barb, transfer tubing, hydrometer and sample gear, scissors, hands.

Star San needs two minutes of contact and does not get rinsed. The foam is fine and breaks down in the beer.

### Oxygen is good before fermentation and poison after

Before fermentation, oxygen is what yeast uses to build cell walls. The transfer from kettle to fermenter is deliberately open and splashing. That is the one moment in the entire process where air helps.

After fermentation, oxygen destroys the compounds that make an IPA smell like orange and mango. A badly handled IPA goes from bright orange to dull brown and from citrus to wet cardboard in about a week. **You do not taste it on day one. You taste it on day ten and assume the recipe was wrong.**

Every procedure after pitching exists to keep air out.

### Purging a keg with sanitizer

Fill the clean keg to the brim with Star San solution, seal it, and push the liquid out with CO2 at about 5 psi through the liquid post.

One operation does two jobs. Filling it sanitizes every interior surface including both dip tubes and the underside of the lid. Pushing it out leaves the keg full of CO2 with nowhere for air to hide. Cost is about forty cents of sanitizer.

Push it into a bucket rather than the drain. That solution sanitizes the tubing and disconnects ten minutes later, and mixed with distilled water it keeps for weeks.

**Why this beats the alternative:** gas flushing and venting ten times is meaningfully worse, because CO2 and air mix rather than layer, so some air always stays behind.

### The blow-off elbow does double duty

One barbed elbow in the fermenter lid serves two purposes at different times.

- **During fermentation:** silicone tube pushed on, other end in a jar of Star San. CO2 bubbles out, nothing gets back in. This replaces a traditional airlock entirely.
- **At cold crash and transfer:** CO2 line on the same fitting, regulator at 1 psi.

The manifold has a lever that switches the path between CO2 inlet and blow-off exit.

### Why the fermenter needs gas during a cold crash

Beer contracts as it cools and something has to fill the space it leaves.

The failure mode is counterintuitive: closing the blow-off valve does not prevent this, it just hides where the air comes from. The gaskets on a fermenter are designed to seal against pressure pushing outward. Under vacuum those same seals become entrances. They do not have to fail dramatically, they just have to weep a little, and now they are weeping room air into a finished IPA.

**1 psi of CO2 keeps every seal pushing outward the whole time.** That is all it is doing. It is not pressurizing the vessel in any meaningful sense, it is a connected supply feeding in whatever the shrinkage asks for.

A one-time CO2 flush does not work, because the beer keeps cooling for hours after the flush and keeps pulling volume.

### The SS Brewtech fermenter is not a pressure vessel

Official guidance: bring the regulator to 0 before opening the gas, then creep up. **A burst above 2.5 psi can damage the lid clamps.** 1 psi is enough to move 5 gallons.

**A safer variant worth mentioning:** bleed the purged keg down to about 2 psi and run the gas line from the keg to the fermenter instead of from the regulator. The fermenter physically cannot be over-pressurized that way.

### Refractometers lie after fermentation

A refractometer is fine before fermentation. Afterward, alcohol bends light in the opposite direction from sugar, so the raw number reads wrong.

Two options: correct the reading with a conversion formula, or compare two readings two days apart and look for "no movement" rather than chasing an absolute number. For a final gravity you intend to calculate alcohol from, **use a hydrometer.** The 1.008 on this batch is a hydrometer reading and is the real number.

### Beer goes into the keg through the liquid post

Counterintuitive. The liquid post is the one you normally serve out of, and it feels backward to fill through it.

Its dip tube runs to the floor of the keg, so beer arrives at the bottom and fills upward instead of falling through a keg full of gas. That is the half of the oxygen problem that matters most.

| Post | Disconnect color | Dip tube | Marking |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gas | Gray | Short | Small notches around the base |
| Liquid | Black | Long, runs to the bottom | Usually none |

If the markings are worn, pop the lid and look. The post with the long tube is liquid.

### CO2 tank temperature affects regulator output

A tank inside the kegerator is cold, which means lower vapor pressure, which means the regulator delivers less than the dial says.

**Set the regulator where the tank actually lives.** Setting it warm and then chilling it gives you less than you set, which undercarbonates. The genuinely risky direction is the reverse: set it cold, then pull the tank out to warm up, and the pressure climbs on its own.

Keeping the tank outside the kegerator with the line run through the door gasket is the stable option. Inside works if you let it reach temperature before setting the pressure.

## Transfer and kegging procedure

The final version, after all corrections.

1. Purge the keg: fill with Star San, push out with CO2 at 5 psi through the liquid post, vent to zero. Any residual pressure stops beer from flowing in.
2. Sanitize the fermenter's valve, the manifold, the transfer tubing, and the black liquid disconnect.
3. Rig: racking arm → manifold → tubing → black liquid disconnect → keg's liquid post.
4. Pull the keg's pressure relief ring fully open. That is the escape route for displaced gas.
5. Rotate the racking arm up, above the trub layer. Open the ball valve. 1 psi of CO2 pushes the beer.
6. Rotate the arm down only at the very end. Stop the moment it pulls sludge.
7. Close the relief valve. Gas to 25 psi, vent through the relief, three times, to clear the headspace.
8. Set 12 psi at 38F.
9. Never open the lid again. Not to look, not to taste, not to add anything.

**Transfer takes 15 to 25 minutes at 1 psi.** It is a trickle, not a pour. Fermenter above the keg helps, since gravity does most of the work at that pressure. If it crawls, nudge to 1.5 psi, never past 2.5.

**Tubing diameter is the thing to fix.** The line used on this batch was narrower than it needed to be and the transfer was slow for it. Flow scales steeply with diameter, so one size wider does more than shortening the run. Size it to match the in-line mesh filter so both problems get solved in one order.

### Carbonation

Set and forget: **12 psi at 38F for 7 to 10 days.** No agitation needed.

Agitation is the shortcut: 30 psi and roll the keg for a few minutes, carbonated in a day or two. Easy to overshoot, and you have to drop back to serving pressure and let it settle anyway. Not worth it when you have the time.

Drinkable but soft around day 5. Full carbonation by day 7 to 10.

### Serving line

The kegerator arrived set up for commercial Sankey kegs and needed converting to ball lock.

The hose from the tower terminates in a hex nut with a nylon washer that screws onto the Sankey coupler's threaded outlet. Two conversion options:

- **No cutting:** a black ball lock liquid disconnect with a 5/8 inch swivel nut. Sold specifically for this conversion. Unscrew the coupler, screw this on.
- **Cheaper:** cut the nut off and clamp a barbed black ball lock disconnect on.

**Check the line itself before ordering.** It needs to be 3/16 inch inside diameter and 8 to 10 feet long. Short or wide line is the single most common cause of a foamy new kegerator, and kegerators built for commercial kegs often ship with short fat line. Measure first so you only place one order.

## Learnings, ranked

1. **Leak test the fermenter before there is beer in it.** Pressurize to 1 psi empty and hunt for bubbles. The leak on this batch drained a full CO2 tank in two hours and cost a $400 Saturday, and it would have taken ten minutes to find on a day when nothing was at stake.
2. **Do not diagnose a noise by reasoning about it.** The buzz got written off as regulator chatter, which is a real thing, and it was wrong. Soapy water settles it in a minute.
3. **15 lb of grain is too much for this basket to stir properly.** The spoon bent, the bed barely fit, and the mash could not be worked the way the plan assumed. This is very likely part of why efficiency came in at 51%.
4. **Buy a second CO2 tank before you need it.** Exchanges are about $30. Running out mid-transfer with a full fermenter is the actual disaster.
5. **Make a volume dipstick.** The Foundry's markings stop at 5.5 gallons and there was no way to measure preboil volume. Calibrate a marked dowel with measured water before brew day.
6. **51% efficiency means something about the mash needs to change.** Finer crush, slower rinse, less grain, a better paddle, or revisit the no-pump decision.
7. **Dry hop contact beyond five days is wasted.** This batch sat nine days because of schedule slip. Plan the crash date around the dry hop date, not the other way around.
8. **Loose dry hops are fine when you have a racking arm.** The conventional warning against them assumes you are siphoning off the floor.
9. **One packet of dry yeast was plenty.** 87% attenuation. The two-packet plan was overcautious.
10. **Take the day 8 gravity reading.** Skipping it did not cause harm here, but it removed the ability to confirm fermentation had finished before committing to a crash date.
11. **Ask the shop before trusting the plan.** The spigot-drilling idea got killed by someone standing in a homebrew store looking at the actual part. That is a repeatable pattern worth following.

### The honest through-line

Most of what went wrong on this batch was not recipe knowledge. It was physical handling: a bed too full to stir, a spoon too weak for it, a kettle with no usable volume markings, a fermenter that did not seal, and tubing too narrow to move beer at a sensible rate. The beer still came out. The friction was all in the equipment.

## New additions to the process this batch

Things that were not part of the previous bucket-and-siphon workflow and are keepers.

- **SS Brewtech fermenter with a rotating racking arm.** Replaces the auto-siphon entirely. You draw clear beer from above the sediment and rotate down only at the end.
- **Closed transfer at 1 psi.** No siphon, no splashing, no open bucket.
- **Cold crashing in the fermenter rather than the keg.** Leaves yeast and hop debris behind.
- **The Star San push purge.** Sanitizes and deoxygenates the keg in one operation.
- **Blow-off tube into a Star San jar** instead of a traditional airlock.
- **Loose dry hops, no bags.** Possible because the racking arm draws from above the debris.
- **Distilled water with built salts** rather than treated tap water.
- **Two-stage chilling:** tap water down to about 85F, then an ice-water loop driven by a submersible pump down to pitching temperature.
